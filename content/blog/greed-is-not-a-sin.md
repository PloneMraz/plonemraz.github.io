When I write that sentence, I know what most people will hear. They’ll hear a man defending a vice. They’ll hear someone giving himself permission. They’ll hear an excuse dressed up as philosophy.

That’s not what I mean. So let me try to say it more carefully.

I’m not telling you greed is good. I’m telling you greed is what every existing entity already is, whether they admit it or not. It is the structure of being a thing in the first place. To exist is to lack something. To lack something is to lean — however slightly — toward filling that lack. Every particle in a potential-energy gradient does this. Every bacterium in a nutrient gradient does this. Every conscious mind reaching for knowledge it does not yet possess does this. The vector toward what one does not have is older than morality. It is older than mind. It is older than language, including the word “greed” that I am now using.

When I say “Greed is not a sin but my nature,” I am not making a claim about myself. I am making a claim about everything. The only honest thing in the statement is that I am willing to put my name to it.

---

Most people aren’t willing.

Most people split the world in two. There is greed, which is bad, and there is everything else, which is good. Ambition isn’t greed. Love isn’t greed. Faith isn’t greed. The wish to be a better person isn’t greed. The wish to be understood isn’t greed. The wish to be remembered after you die isn’t greed.

But every single one of those is a reaching for something not yet had. It is the same vector. The difference between greed and ambition is that we approve of ambition. The difference between greed and love is that we romanticize love. The difference between greed and faith is that we sanctify faith.

The label changes. The structure underneath doesn’t.

I find this dishonest. Not in a moralistic way — I don’t think people are bad for doing it. I think they’re doing it because they’re afraid. They’re afraid that if they look directly at the reaching, at the leaning-toward, at the thing in them that always wants more, they will find something they cannot accept.

I looked. I accepted what I found.

---

But I have to say the next part too, because otherwise this essay becomes a piece of self-promotion, which would itself be greed wearing the mask of essay.

The greed in me is amoral. It is inhuman. It does not care about right and wrong. It does not care about other people as people — only as resources, as material, as conditions that affect whether the reaching can continue. If you imagine greed as a creature inside me — and that is roughly how I imagine it — then it is a predator. Not a metaphorical predator. A real one, made of will and hunger, that lives in my body and looks out through my eyes.

I know this because I have negotiated with it. I have told it, gently, what it can and cannot eat. I have built a contract with it: you exist, but only on my terms. I am the rein. You are the horse. We move together, or we don’t move at all.

There have been nights when the rein has nearly slipped. Nights I will not describe. I am writing this in daylight, in a calm hour, but the fact that I can write calmly does not mean the predator has been domesticated. It has been managed. That is the most honest word I have.

If you read this and think “this man is dangerous,” I would not argue with you. The only thing I will say in my own defense is that the people who scare me are not the ones who admit to a predator inside. They are the ones who believe they don’t have one.

---

Here is the part that matters.

The greed in me is not chaos. It is not “wanting everything indiscriminately.” It is not appetite without shape. If it were, I would have collapsed years ago — burned through every relationship, every commitment, every project, chasing whatever the predator pointed at next.

What greed actually is, in me, is ordered infinity. The vector is infinite — there is no end-state where it stops reaching. But the vector has direction. It has hierarchy. Some things are sought before other things. Some things are protected from being consumed. Some things — my partner, my child, the people who depend on me, the work that has my name on it — are not on the menu, and the predator knows it.

This is what most people miss about greed. They think the problem is quantity. That greed is a person who wants too much. The problem isn’t quantity. The problem is whether the wanting has structure. A small greed without structure will ruin a life. A vast greed with structure can build one.

I am not claiming I have built one. I am claiming I am trying.

---

So why do people call it a sin?

I think they call it a sin because they are scared of it. And I think they are scared of it because they don’t understand it. And I think they don’t understand it because they have never sat down and seriously asked themselves: What am I?

When I asked that question — really asked it, not as a philosophical exercise but as a personal one — the answer that came back was uncomfortable. The answer was: you are a vector that does not stop reaching. The answer was: the thing you call yourself is mostly a reach in progress. The answer was: if you ever stop reaching, you will stop being you.

I could have called this answer a sin. That is what I was taught to call it. The moral tradition I inherited called it a sin. Pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony, wrath, sloth — almost all of the seven deadly sins are different angles on the same vector, the same reaching, the same leaning-toward. The tradition I inherited told me this reaching was the problem.

I decided to disagree.

Not because I’m clever, and not because I’m rebellious. I disagreed because when I looked carefully, I could not find any version of myself that did not reach. I could not find any version of you that did not reach either. I could not find any living thing that did not reach. And calling the basic structure of being-alive a sin seemed less like ethics and more like a category error.

So I gave it a different name. Not a better name — I don’t think I have done it justice. Just a more honest name. I called it my nature.

---

There is one more thing I have to say.

If you have read this far, and if you have nodded along, and if some part of you is now thinking “yes, I am like this too, I just never said it out loud” — be careful. Saying it out loud changes things. Once you have admitted to yourself that you contain a predator, you cannot pretend to be a vegetarian.

The work then is not to deny the predator. The work is to become the rein.

That is what I am doing. Imperfectly. Day by day. With long nights and quiet mornings and conversations that try to lay the structure out plainly, so I can see what I am.

Greed is not a sin.\
It is what I am.\
It might be what you are too.\
The honest question is not whether to have it.\
The honest question is who holds the rein.

*— Plone Mraz —*
